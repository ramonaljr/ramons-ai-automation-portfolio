**The short answer:** automated payment reminders work when fixed policy rules decide who gets chased and how firmly, and AI only writes the wording. Add a database rule that allows one reminder row per invoice, so nothing sends twice. Read customer replies and route them. And make a dispute stop every reminder until a person clears it, with no timer and no automatic restart.

## What goes wrong with manual chasing

Chasing unpaid invoices by hand is inconsistent. Some invoices get a reminder every day, others none at all. The failures that damage relationships are the wrong reminders:

- A customer who **disputed** the invoice keeps getting chased.
- A customer who **promised a payment date** keeps getting chased.
- A customer who **already paid** gets a reminder anyway.
- **Replies sit unread** in an inbox while the next reminder goes out.
- A **harsh final notice** lands on a key client and surprises the account manager.

Every one of those is a failure of context, not effort. The person sending the reminder did not know what the rest of the company knew. So the first job of automation here is to gather that context before anything is sent.

I built a workflow for this: the [AI Unpaid Invoice Reminders](/case-study/ai-unpaid-invoice-reminders) build. It is a complete working workflow for a fictional sample client, TalentBridge Staffing, and not a delivered client engagement. It is the worked example for the rest of this article.

## Gather the context from three places

The workflow runs every morning and loads its data once per run:

- **Xero** for the unpaid invoices and each customer's billing email.
- **Pipedrive** for the account owner and any open deals.
- **Postgres** for the reminder history.

Reminder history is the one people forget. Without it, the workflow has no memory, so it cannot know that it sent a reminder yesterday or that this customer already received a final notice.

## Rules decide, AI writes

This is the principle the whole build rests on: **who gets a reminder, when, and how firmly is decided by company policy, never by the AI.**

The rules skip disputed invoices, wrong contacts and promised dates. They decide how firm the reminder should be as the invoice gets later. Only then does the AI get involved, and only to write the email.

The AI is told to stay inside the approved policy: firmer the later the invoice is, with no invented fees, no accusations and no legal threats. The subject line is built by rules, not by the model. That restriction is the safety feature. A model that can set its own tone can threaten a customer on your behalf, and you will find out from the customer.

The general lesson is one I keep coming back to in [error handling is the whole job](/blog/error-handling-is-the-whole-job): keep deterministic decisions in deterministic code, and let the model do the part that is genuinely about language.

## One row per invoice, enforced by the database

A reminder must never go out twice. The cleanest way to guarantee that is to make it impossible rather than unlikely.

Each reminder is recorded in Postgres, where **one row per invoice is enforced.** If the workflow runs twice, because it was retriggered or because a previous run failed halfway, the database refuses the second write, so the second reminder is never sent.

That is idempotency done at the right layer. Code that checks "did I already send this?" can have a bug. A constraint in the database cannot be forgotten.

## The final notice is a human decision first

At 30 or more days late, the workflow does something deliberately cautious. It tells the account owner in Slack and creates a Pipedrive task first. Only one final notice ever goes out, and after that the account owner takes over.

This answers the problem of the surprised account manager. The person who owns the relationship hears about the final notice before the customer does, and they can step in if the customer is a key client.

## Read the replies, and treat them as instructions

Reminders only matter if replies are read. In this build, AI reads each customer reply and pulls out the details that matter: promised dates, invoice numbers, dispute reasons and new contacts. Then the reply is routed.

- A **promised payment** sets the next reminder date.
- **"Already paid"** triggers a check in Xero.
- A **request for an invoice copy** is handled as a request, not as a dispute.
- A **wrong contact** updates who gets the reminders.
- A **question** goes to a person.
- A **dispute** stops everything.

The system understands seven reply types. The list is not what matters. What matters is that a reply changes what happens next, instead of sitting unread while a timer sends another email.

## A dispute stops everything, with no timer

This is the safeguard I would defend hardest. When a reply is a dispute, **all reminders stop until a person clears it.** There is no timer and no automatic restart. A Pipedrive task, a Slack alert and the saved emails make sure a person actually picks it up.

The design question is what should happen if nobody picks it up. Most systems answer "resume after some days". That is exactly how a customer who disputed an invoice receives a collections email a week later. Here the answer is that the reminders stay stopped, and the cost of that choice is a visible, assigned task.

## When something fails

Failures go to Slack and an error log. Because of the one-row-per-invoice rule, tomorrow's run simply picks up where the failed one stopped, without double-sending. A broken run leaves a visible alert and a safe state, and not a customer who got three emails.

## What the team still does

Automation does not remove the people. In this build the team only handles disputes, questions, and final notices to important clients. Every overdue invoice is followed up consistently by policy, and every reply is read and acted on the same day. The work that remains is the work that needs judgment.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can AI send payment reminders automatically?

Yes, but the AI should only write the wording. Fixed policy rules should decide which invoices get a reminder, when, and how firm it is, because a model deciding its own timing and tone can chase the wrong customer or threaten one. Rules for the decision and AI for the language is the safe split.

### How do you stop reminders to a customer who disputes an invoice?

Read the customer's replies and classify them. When a reply is a dispute, stop all reminders for that invoice until a person clears it, and create a task and an alert so someone owns it. Avoid an automatic restart timer, since that is how a disputed invoice ends up chased again.

### How do you make sure a payment reminder is never sent twice?

Enforce it in the database. Record each reminder in a table that allows only one row per invoice, so a second write is refused even if the workflow runs twice. A database constraint is more reliable than code that checks whether a reminder was already sent.

### What is dunning, and is it the same as invoice reminders?

Dunning is the process of following up on overdue payments, typically with a series of reminders that grow firmer over time. Automated invoice reminders are a form of dunning. The risk with a rigid series is that it ignores context such as a dispute or a promised date, which is why the rules need to see the customer's history.

**The short answer:** you can automate invoice approval safely if the AI only reads and fixed rules decide. Check for duplicates before and after extraction, match every invoice against its purchase order and delivery record with plain arithmetic, send exceptions to accounts payable, and make a person approve every bill before it reaches the ledger. Automation then removes the matching and the chasing, not the control.

## Why invoice approval breaks when it is done by hand

In most accounts payable teams the week looks the same. Invoices arrive by email. Someone opens each PDF, finds the purchase order, finds the delivery record, compares three documents, and then asks someone else to approve it.

That process fails in predictable ways:

- **Matching is slow,** so it gets skipped when the team is busy.
- **The same invoice arrives twice,** in a new email, and gets paid twice.
- **Approvers see a PDF,** not the problems, so they approve what looks plausible.
- **There is no single record** of what was posted, rejected or held.

Construction makes it worse, because progress payments invite specific mistakes: paying for the same work twice, paying in full when retention should be held back, or paying without a signed lien waiver. I built a workflow for exactly that case, so this article uses it as the worked example. It is a complete working build for a fictional sample client, [Braewick Construction Group](/case-study/ai-invoice-processing-approval), and not a delivered client engagement.

## Step 1: pick up the invoice, and be honest about scans

The workflow checks Gmail every five minutes for unread emails with a PDF attached. That is deliberately boring. A polling schedule is easy to reason about, easy to monitor, and easy to restart.

One decision here saves money and avoids bad data: if the PDF is a scanned image with no readable text, it does not go to the AI. It goes to the team. A model asked to read an image of nothing will produce something anyway, and that something looks exactly like a real invoice. Sending unreadable documents to a person costs a minute. Sending them to a model risks a confident, wrong number in your ledger.

## Step 2: check for duplicates twice

Duplicate payment is the cheapest mistake to prevent and the most embarrassing to explain, so the workflow checks twice.

1. **Before the AI reads anything,** each email and file is checked against the invoice log. This catches the same invoice re-sent, and it happens before any AI cost.
2. **After the AI reads it,** the workflow checks again by vendor and invoice number. This catches the case where the same invoice arrives as a different file or in a differently worded email.

Two checks sound redundant until you realise they catch different failures. The first is about the message. The second is about the invoice itself.

## Step 3: let the AI read, and never let it decide

This is the line I will not move. The model's job is extraction: vendor, purchase order and job numbers, cost code, invoice type, billing to date, amounts already approved, retention and lien waiver. It is told never to invent a value that is not on the page.

Everything after that is deterministic. Arithmetic, tolerances and routing are not a language model's job, because **a model has no failure mode where it stops.** It always returns something. I wrote about the general version of that in [error handling is the whole job](/blog/error-handling-is-the-whole-job), and invoices are where it matters most.

The one place the model's own uncertainty is used is a confidence check. If the AI is less than 80% sure it read the invoice correctly, a person confirms the details in Slack before anything else happens. The threshold is a policy choice. What matters is that low confidence has somewhere to go that is not the ledger.

## Step 4: fixed rules do the three-way match

A three-way match compares the invoice with the purchase order and with the record that the goods or work were actually delivered. If all three agree, the bill is probably right.

The rules in this build are plain and fixed:

- Amounts are within **2%** of the purchase order and delivery record.
- Billing to date **adds up**, including amounts already approved.
- Progress payments **stay within the contract value**.
- **Retention is held back.**
- A **lien waiver is attached.**

None of that involves a model. It is comparison and addition against records in Airtable. That is why an auditor can read it: every rejection has a rule behind it that you can point to.

## Step 5: approval in Slack, routed by what was found

Where an invoice goes depends on what the rules found:

- **Clean invoices** go to the budget owner.
- **Invoices with issues** go to accounts payable, with the issues already flagged.

Either way, a person decides. The workflow waits up to two days for an answer, and **nothing reaches QuickBooks without that approval.** Approvers no longer open a PDF and hunt for what is wrong. They see the invoice, the match result and the specific problems, and they approve or reject in the same message.

This is the part finance leads care about most, and it is why I describe the system as automating the decision while gating the consequence. The workflow does the matching. A person owns the money.

## Step 6: post at the right amount, and never guess

Approved bills are added to QuickBooks. Two details matter:

- **The amount is the amount to pay after retention,** not the face value of the invoice.
- **A missing vendor is never guessed.** If the vendor cannot be found in QuickBooks, the bill is held and the team is alerted. Creating a vendor from a guess is how you end up paying a lookalike.

Every outcome is logged, including rejections and holds, and the email is marked as read. Failures go to Slack and an error log with the invoice flagged for manual entry, so a broken run produces a visible to-do and not a silent gap.

## What I would want you to know before building this

A few honest limits:

- The 2% tolerance and the 80% confidence threshold are **policy decisions.** Your numbers will differ, and they should be set with whoever owns the budget.
- The rules above are written for construction progress payments. A different industry needs different rules, and writing them is most of the work.
- This is a sample build for a fictional client. The workflow is real and complete, but I am not claiming results from a live finance team.

What carries over to any industry is the shape: duplicate checks, extraction without judgment, fixed rules, a person approving, and a log of everything.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a three-way match in accounts payable?

A three-way match compares an invoice with its purchase order and with the record that the goods or work were delivered. If the three agree within a set tolerance, the invoice can be approved. In an automated version the comparison is done by fixed rules against your records, so every mismatch has a stated reason.

### Can AI approve invoices on its own?

It should not. AI is good at reading a messy PDF into structured fields, but approval is a decision with financial consequences. The safer design lets AI extract, lets fixed rules check the numbers, and requires a person to approve each bill before it reaches the ledger.

### How do you prevent duplicate invoice payments in an automated workflow?

Check more than once. Compare the incoming email and file against an invoice log before spending anything on extraction, then compare vendor and invoice number after the invoice has been read. The first catches re-sent messages, and the second catches the same invoice arriving in a different form.
